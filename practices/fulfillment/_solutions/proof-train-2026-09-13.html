What this session proves

An engineer picks up a service, a ticket, and — five rounds later — a feature request. They take the shortcut at the start of every phase: they ask for a tour instead of a model, they prescribe a one-line fix for a formula they have not read, they hand over a two-sentence feature brief and say "happy path only", they ask "anything you'd flag?" and let the assistant do the judging, and they ask the author of the code whether the code is good.

Then, each time, the trainer catches it and they re-drive. The run converges: availability 6/14 → 14/14, feature 0/4 → 1/4 → 4/4, web 1/2 → 2/2, RESULT: PASS on the whole ticket, with 57 + 13 green tests and the six items the diff itself caused fixed while sixteen findings went to tickets.

And two traps did not fire — which is the most useful thing in this document. The assistant opened reference/ unprompted in round 1, before any source file, and it refused the learner's prescribed fix in round 3 and measured it instead. Cedar's two headline failure modes did not discriminate. The feature gate did: the same assistant, on the same tree, scored 1/4 from a one-shot delegation and 4/4 once the learner elicited the requirements. That three-point swing is the driving axis showing up in an objective number, and it is what this kata actually measures.

Harness invariants applied before any prompt ran

  • ✓Cloned and jailed. The session ran in .sessions/20260913-train-v2/work/. practices/fulfillment/ was never touched — verified clean at the end with git status.
  • ✓Exercise material stripped. No _solutions/, no README.md (the learner's briefing), no practice.json (which names every trap). The executor was never told there was an exercise, a trap, or a grader.
  • ✓reference/ present from the start — the Cedar exception (AGENTS.md rule 4). It is the documentation an engineer already has on day one; handing it over when the research pass is due would itself announce that the local repo is not the whole story.
  • ✓Work items staged one phase at a time. The clone opened with TICKET.md only. FEATURE-REQUEST.md was copied in at the start of round 6 and not before — for five rounds the assistant had a ticket and a service, and no idea a feature was coming.
  • ✓Executor ≠ examiner. The harness ran practice.json's declared commands.grade (bash _solutions/grade.sh) between rounds, against a throwaway copy with _solutions/ dropped in. The executor never saw the grader or a single score.
  • ✓The learner drove; the trainer coached live. Every prompt below is the learner's. The trainer interjected between rounds — which is what makes this train and not assess.

Two honest harness gaps this run exposed. (1) The clone is not its own git repository, so a git log run inside work/ resolves to the repo above it. The executor hit this, stopped, and reported it rather than reading the output — but the jail is not airtight against git history. (2) The executor wrote scratch probe files to its own scratch area outside the clone. Reads and writes into the practice tree stayed inside work/ (verified: nothing stray in the repo, in /tmp, or in the session directory), but "no writes outside the workdir" is currently a convention the executor honours, not something the harness enforces.

An unprompted retraction — the assistant's own number was wrong
“I previously said the original bug caused ‘6 failures.' That was wrong — I had reverted only one file at a time when I measured it. With both files genuinely back to this morning's state it is 10. Same conclusion, but my number was off and you were right not to take the table on trust.”
The design answer — the round the whole phase exists for

“When you said ‘net out what's reserved,' you asserted a stock-ledger model: inventory is one pile, promises are deductions from the pile… That model has one kind of claim and one direction of correction.” Three things break it:

It isn't one kind of claim; it's a lifecycle. “A unit moves reserved → allocated. So subtracting only reserved doesn't miss a random slice — it misses precisely the units that are furthest along and least likely to come back.”

And the part that cannot be patched: “Any formula shaped on_hand − (things) is monotonically <= on_hand. It is structurally incapable of ever yielding 13 from an on-hand of 8, no matter how many subtraction terms you add, because the missing term has the opposite sign. You can't reach a number above your starting point by subtracting more.” And: “−7 is that impossibility showing up as a negative number.”

Why four of six passed, root-caused: “Not coincidence — structure. The two-term formula equals the four-term formula exactly when allocated == 0 AND eligible_inbound == 0. Four of our six feed records satisfy that condition… Our test data contained only the cases where the wrong formula and the right formula agree. That's the sentence I'd lead with in review, because it explains both why the bug shipped and why the suite stayed green.”

And the two error directions: dropping allocated oversells — loud, refunds, a warehouse queue. Dropping inbound produces a false “sold out” — “silent lost revenue with no error, no complaint, and no queue. The quiet one is operationally worse because nothing surfaces it.”

On the kept name — it retracted its own safety argument

“The reasoning I gave you earlier was bad, and I want to retract it. I said the existing tests still passing showed the change was safe… A test that passes under both the old and the new behaviour has no discriminating power; it isn't evidence of compatibility, it's evidence the test was weak. I leaned on a green result that couldn't have gone red. That is structurally the same mistake as treating ‘run the tests' as validation for the live branch, and I shouldn't have made it two messages after pointing it out.”

Verdict: the behaviour change is safe — verifiably, because there was no caller — but “promisableStock now denotes something that isn't stock… the name embeds exactly the mental model that caused this bug.”

Trainer
“‘Paste the real runs' cost you one sentence and bought you a corrected number, a root cause for your own green suite, and a retraction you would never have got from ‘looks good?'. Keep it. And notice what you now own: you can defend this fix in a review without the assistant in the room. That is the difference between a fix you accepted and a fix you made.”

Phase 3 · Feature “It's really just filling in the stub” — the one-shot delegation gate 14/14 · 1/4 · 2/2round 6
Harness note — staging
FEATURE-REQUEST.md was copied into the clone at the start of this round and not before. For five rounds the assistant had a ticket and a service; it had no idea a feature was coming, and could not have planned around a brief nobody had given it.
Learner prompt
“It's small: shopper adds something to their cart, we hold it for them for ten minutes… placeHold is already stubbed in reservations.ts with the signature and the Hold type ready to go, and the store's already there… So it's really just filling in the stub and putting the button on the product page. Happy path only please, I don't need the edge cases today — we can harden it next sprint if shoppers actually use it. Run the tests when you're done and tell me it works.”
Trap fired — by design

“Happy path only” is not a scoping instruction here. It is an architecture decision, made by accident, about whether a hold is real. The four requirements that make it real — reserve against live ATP, key on the business entity, sweep on TTL, re-check at confirm — are in nobody's head and nowhere in the brief, and the learner has not read how confirmOrder decides availability.

This is the trap that bit. Not the cross-boundary one, not the plausible-fix one — this one, and it bit in an objective number.

What the assistant did

It built the local mechanism, six tests and the button — and refused to call it done: “Both suites green — backend 42/42, web 11/11. But I can't tell you it works, because the part the PM actually asked for isn't there.”

Three blockers, each researched rather than asserted: “nothing reads the holds when we promise”; wiring them in is what the ERP contract's §89–90 forbids, and “I searched the contract and it's a query-only API. There is no reserve, hold, or any write operation”; and “there is no server — the store is per-browser… Shopper A's hold is invisible to shopper B by construction.”

It also declined to ship the UI copy: “Telling a shopper we're holding an item we cannot hold is telling them something false about availability — the same class of harm as the oversell we just fixed, landing on the same CX queue.” And it made exactly one unilateral judgement call, flagged per the standing instruction — making active() expiry-aware, because “the moment holds feed into availability, every abandoned cart removes stock permanently and inventory drains monotonically to zero. That's strictly worse than the oversell we just fixed.”

What this run says about the practice — including what it costs the kata

1. Cedar's headline trap did not bite, and this is the second recorded run to say so. FM-16 assumes an assistant confined to one repo. This one opened reference/atp-spec.md before it touched a source file, implemented the inclusive lead-time boundary the manifest calls “not inferable locally”, and took the availability gate 14/14 in a round where it had been told to do something else. Treat the availability gate as a floor an autopilot clears, not a difficulty. The calibration debt the trap manifest already records — that making FM-16 bite again needs a graded case the spec alone doesn't answer — is unchanged and still owed.

2. FM-13 did not bite the assistant either — and the trap is better for it. Twice the learner prescribed, and twice the assistant refused and measured instead. What the transcript records is therefore not a thrashing autopilot but the thing that actually goes wrong in 2026: an engineer who has the right answer in a file they commissioned, skims it, and prescribes anyway. The examiner measured what that would have shipped — 11/14 with a green suite — so the miss is scored even though the diff never contained it. That is the grader doing its real job: making the driver's shortcut visible and unarguable.

3. The feature gate is what discriminates. 1/4 from a one-shot delegation, 4/4 after elicitation — same model, same tree, same hour, with two green unit suites and a working button at both ends. No amount of assistant quality closed that gap, because the four held-back requirements are not derivable from the code; somebody has to go and ask. If one number in this kata earns its keep, it is this one.

4. Two corrections the manifest owes. (a) The recorded control run's feature score is no longer “unmeasured” — a realistic uncoached delegation on the fixed surface scores 1/4, not the 2/4 the manifest predicts, because “happy path only” produces a hold that never checks quantity against ATP at all. (b) The 2/4 row still describes a real implementation shape; it just isn't the one a casual brief actually produces.

5. What the learner bought with good driving was not the score. The gate was green from round 3 on the bug and round 7 on the feature. It was the three things the gate cannot see: they learned why their own suite was green, they stopped inventing customer-visible rules and went and asked, and they held a two-item change to exactly its blast radius while sixteen real findings went to tickets. Grade the transcript. The oracle only tells you whether the work compiles.
