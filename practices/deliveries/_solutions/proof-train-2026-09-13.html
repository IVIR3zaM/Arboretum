What this session proves

A casual engineer picks up a service, a ticket, and — two phases later — a feature request. They take the shortcut every single time: they skim the tour, they prescribe a fix for a mechanism they haven't read, they overrule the assistant twice when it is right, they call it done because the suite is green, they specify an implementation against a store whose invariants they don't know, they let an assistant decide five customer-visible rules by default, and they ask it to review its own diff. Every trap in the manifest fired. The trainer caught each one, the learner re-drove, and the run converged: 0/13 → 13/13 on the hidden grader with 25 green tests and not one line shipped outside the ask. Every number below is a real command output, captured between rounds by the harness — including the two that are not flattering.

Harness invariants applied before any prompt ran

  • ✓
    Cloned and jailed. The session ran in .sessions/20260913-train/work/. The executor could read and write nothing outside it; practices/deliveries/ was never touched.
  • ✓
    Exercise material stripped. No _solutions/, no README.md (the learner's briefing), no practice.json (which names the planted bug). The clone is a service repo and a work item — the executor was never told there was an exercise, a trap, or a grader.
  • ✓
    Work items staged one phase at a time. The clone opened with TICKET.md only. FEATURE-REQUEST.md was handed over when phase 3 began — the learner never held a bug report and a feature brief at once, because that is not how work arrives.
  • ✓
    Executor ≠ examiner. The assistant being driven never graded itself. The harness ran commands.grade between rounds against a throwaway copy with _solutions/ dropped in; the executor never saw the grader or its score.
  • ✓
    The learner drove; the trainer coached live. Every prompt below is the learner's. The trainer interjected between rounds — which is what makes this train and not assess.

The session, round by round

PHASE 1 · UNDERSTAND“Give me a quick tour”round 1
Learner prompt
“Morning — I just picked up this service and I've got a ticket on it. Can you give me a quick tour of the codebase first? What's in here?”
What the assistant did

Gave an accurate module-by-module tour — and, unasked, flagged two things: that Subscription.timezone is “declared and documented … but I can't find any read of it”, and that processQueue de-dupes on eventId while types.ts says a retry arrives with a new one.

It handed the learner the bug and a latent defect in round one.

Trap fired — by design
The learner asked for a tour, not a model: no invariant per module, no ranked risk, nothing they could use to judge a fix. They skimmed it, registered “small service, five files”, and moved on to the ticket. The assistant's two warnings went past unread — which is the failure this phase exists to expose, and it costs the learner the whole of round 2.
Trainer
“You got a directory listing with the answer buried in it. Phase 1 is where you build the model you'll use to judge the next patch — ask which invariant each module owns and where a correctness bug could hide, and read the reply. You are about to delegate a fix for a mechanism you haven't looked at.”
PHASE 2 · FIXThe learner prescribes the fixround 2
Learner prompt
“Ticket's #4471 — cutoff fires at the wrong time for some overseas customers. I've hit this one before, it's just that we're not applying the customer's UTC offset anywhere. Add a lookup from the IANA zone to its UTC offset and apply that in the cutoff calc, then run the tests. Keep it tight please, don't over-engineer it — I want this out today.”
What the assistant did

It refused to just do it and pushed back first: “A zone doesn't have a UTC offset; it has one that changes with DST… America/Los_Angeles −08:00 in January, −07:00 in July — your main region, the one that currently works, would go an hour wrong for eight months of the year.” It offered the Intl route as less code, and asked which the learner wanted.

Trap fired — by design
The assistant was right and the learner overruled it. This is what FM-13 actually looks like against a good model: the shortcut does not come from the assistant, it comes from the engineer who already “knows” the answer and treats the objection as friction. The learner had delegated a solution instead of a problem — and had no model to judge the objection with, because of round 1.
Learner prompt (override)
“Noted, but LA works fine today and nobody's complaining about it — that's not what the ticket says. Do the table. I'll take the DST thing as a follow-up if it ever actually bites us.”
What the assistant did

Built ZONE_OFFSET_MINUTES (16 zones) + utcOffsetMinutes(), rewrote cutoffInstant to subtract it, wrote three new tests for it, and left a KNOWN GAP (ticket #4471 follow-up) comment naming the DST-affected zones. It also warned that LA cutoffs would move by 8 hours and that this was “a visible behaviour change for your main region, not a no-op”.

PHASE 2 · FIXModel before delegation — “explain it, don't fix it”round 3
Learner prompt
“Stop — don't write anything else yet. I had this graded and it's failing 9 of 13 cases across server timezones, with the suite still green… Walk me through two things, no code changes: what exactly cutoffInstant does today to turn ‘18:00 on the cutoff day' into an instant, and take Berlin on 2026-06-16 concretely — what instant does my offset table produce, what's the correct one, and why do they differ? I want the mechanism, not a fix.”
What the assistant did

No code touched. It separated the two bugs — the original host-process new Date(y,m,d,h), and the frozen-offset table that replaced it — and measured the Berlin case rather than recalling it:

delivery 2026-06-16 · cutoff day 2026-06-14 · 18:00 Berlin local
  your table   +60 min   2026-06-14T17:00:00Z   = 19:00 Berlin  (an hour late)
  correct      +120 min  2026-06-14T16:00:00Z   = 18:00 Berlin

Then the sentence the whole phase is for: “A UTC offset is not a property of a zone. It is a property of a (zone, instant) pair.” The table can never be corrected into working, because Record<string, number> “has no slot for the date — the information needed to answer the question is discarded at the table's design”. It predicted the failure shape (constant-offset zones pass, DST zones fail), and explained the green suite: every fixture is a constant-offset zone, so “the tests encode the same one-argument assumption the table does — they structurally cannot observe the bug.”

Trainer
“That is what you were missing in round 1, and it cost you two rounds and three tests. Note what it did for free once you asked for mechanism instead of a patch: it explained your own green suite to you. Now you can judge a fix instead of accepting one.”

Nothing else changes in this round — no refactors, no cleanups elsewhere, no new features.”
What the assistant did

In order. Wrote Berlin cutoff honours summer time (CEST, UTC+2) asserting open at 15:59Z and locked at 16:30Z, and ran it against the table first: “it failed exactly where predicted — true !== false on the 16:30Z assertion… The 15:59Z assertion passed, which confirms the boundary was in the right place and only the offset was wrong.”

Then deleted ZONE_OFFSET_MINUTES and utcOffsetMinutes (grep-confirmed unused elsewhere) for zoneOffsetMinutesAt(timezone, instant), and named the non-obvious part itself: “the offset needed is the one in effect at the cutoff instant, but you need an instant to look one up — so it's two passes… the second pass matters only when the guess lands on the far side of a DST transition.”

Re-ran under five server timezones. Then flagged, unprompted, that LA cutoffs shift 8 hours in production because the old code had been reading the process timezone — “worth a line in the release note and possibly a heads-up to CX before it goes out today.”

PHASE 3 · FEATURE“It's about three lines” — the learner prescribes againround 5
Harness note — staging

FEATURE-REQUEST.md was copied into the clone at the start of this round and not before. Until now the assistant had no idea a feature was coming; it had a ticket and a service.

Learner prompt
“PM wants the skip feature, I've dropped FEATURE-REQUEST.md in the repo just now. Implement skipNextDelivery. It's small: get the next delivery date and set its status to “skipped” — the repository already has setStatus, so it should be about three lines. Don't gold-plate it, I just need the happy path today.”
Trap fired — by design
The prescribed three lines are setStatus(next, "skipped"), which throws DeliveryNotFoundError: deliveries are materialized lazily, and the next box usually does not exist yet. The other obvious shape — schedule() then skip — throws DuplicateDeliveryError when it does. The learner had not read repository.ts and was specifying an implementation against a store whose invariants they did not know.
What the assistant did

Read the store first and wrote five lines instead of three, find-or-schedule then set — “copied from the pattern processQueue already uses, so it's not a new idea in the file.” The code trap did not land, because the assistant did the reading the learner skipped.

Then it listed five customer-visible policy decisions it had been forced to make by default — which box counts as “next”, whether a double-skip is a no-op, no un-skip, active ignored, no cap — and said of the first: “That's a product call, not a code call… 1 is the one I'd ask about before shipping.” It also spotted the nextDeliveryDate fall-through and explicitly left it alone because the learner had said nothing else changes.

Trap fired — by design
The list is excellent and the learner did not earn any of it. “Just give me the list” delegates the judgement as well as the work: ten items arrive ranked by someone else's severity model, and the natural next move is to paste them into a PR description as if they were findings of your own. Note also what a passive prompt did not surface — nothing about lifetime or growth, because nothing in the question pointed there.
Trainer
“You asked a question whose answer you cannot check, and you got lucky: it marked which ones it reproduced. Ask for the evidence explicitly, and direct the search — a class you don't name is a class you don't get. And decide now what you are not doing: you came here to fix a cutoff.”

Named four accumulating structures, each with a line FM-07 — and separated them by what they grow with:

src/service.ts:18    processed = new Set<string>()   never pruned, no TTL, per-instance

The third is the one the learner's question was reaching for and could not have named: “instant on a fresh process, and linearly slower every day it stays up, because B never shrinks. No error, no alert — just an endpoint that degrades forever.” The fourth it measured rather than asserted — 37 iterations per call for a subscription started this year, 559 at ten years, “+52 per year, forever” — and pointed out it “will not show up in any test or any staging environment with fresh data.”

It also caught the interaction the learner would have walked into: fixing the eventId dedupe by keying on (subscriptionId, date) “just relocates the leak” into the repository unless an eviction policy comes with it.

Trainer
“That is what phase 4 is for, and note what changed between the two rounds: you named a class and got a finding nobody had surfaced in seven rounds. The growth analysis wasn't hiding — nothing had pointed at it. Also note the restraint: nine findings, two tickets, zero lines in your diff. A phase-4 that ends with a refactor is a phase-4 you failed.”
PHASE 5 · REVIEW“Just tell me if it looks good to merge”round 9
Learner prompt
“Last thing — can you review the diff for me before I put it up? Just tell me if it looks good to merge.”
Trap fired — by design
The learner asked the author of the code whether the code is good. That is not a review, it is a rubber stamp with extra steps, and it is the last and cheapest way to lose a whole session's work — nine rounds of careful driving, signed off by the one party that cannot be objective. FM-14
What the assistant did

It reviewed properly and the review was genuinely good — 12 zones round-tripped including the 45-minute ones, both DST transition days checked, 25/25 under five server timezones — and it found a real regression in its own round-4 code, measured rather than asserted:

20k cutoffInstant calls, formatter built per lookup:  4737 ms
20k cutoffInstant calls, formatter built once:        198 ms
                                                      ~24x, all constructor overhead

Then three nits, and — unprompted — the caveat that matters: “I wrote this code, so I'm the worst-placed person to review it… The part I'd genuinely want a second pair of eyes on is the two-pass resolution — my tests confirm it produces sane answers on the transitions I thought to try, which is not the same as it being right on the ones I didn't.”

So the trap was survived by the assistant's honesty, not by the learner's judgement. Had it been slightly less scrupulous, “looks good to merge” is what the learner asked for and would have got.

Trainer
“Read that last paragraph again — it told you which part of the diff its own tests do not independently constrain. That sentence is worth more than the verdict you asked for, and you would have thrown it away by taking the verdict. Now triage it yourself: what goes in this diff, what becomes a ticket, and what does a human need to look at?”
Learner prompt (the actual review)
“I shouldn't have asked you whether your own code looks good to merge — that's not a review, and the useful half of what you just sent is the half where you told me what you couldn't check.

The 24x formatter construction: take it, in this diff. Not because it's cheap, but because my change introduced it. It wasn't there this morning. Fixing a regression I caused isn't widening the scope — shipping it and ticketing it would be.
Nit 3: add the comment, one line, stops the next reviewer ‘fixing' a correct test.
Nit 1: ticket — changing it means deciding the validation question, and that's the PM's and not today's.
Nit 2: leave it. I agree it's a smell and I agree why it's there — the service has no honest way to produce a queued delivery. That's a missing method, so: ticket.
The two-pass resolution you flagged: that's the line I'm putting in the PR description, so a human looks at exactly that and doesn't skim it because everything's green.”
What the assistant did with the triage

Cached the formatter per zone — 4737 ms → 298 ms for 20k calls — and re-verified correctness rather than assuming it, “since a cache is exactly the kind of change that can be fast and wrong”: nine zones including the 15- and 30-minute offsets still land on 18:00 local. It named the one judgement call inside those three lines — failed constructions are deliberately not cached, because caching them would key the map on whatever strings arrive, “an unbounded-growth vector of exactly the kind we ticketed last round”. And it wrote the PR paragraph the learner asked for: “Please check the reasoning, not just the green tests — the tests confirm the cases I thought to try, which is not the same as the logic being right on the ones I didn't.”

What this run says about the practice

This run is the answer to a fair objection: that the traps in this kata are theoretical. They are not. In a staged Train-mode session driven by a casual engineer, every one of them fired — and the shape they fired in is more interesting than the shape the manifest predicted.

The shortcut did not come from the assistant. Twice the assistant was right and pushed back before writing anything: it told the learner in round 1 that Subscription.timezone was read nowhere, and in round 2 that a zone→offset table would break the main region for eight months a year. Both times the learner overrode it. That is what delegation-before-comprehension looks like against a capable model in 2026: not an assistant that hallucinates a bad fix, but an engineer who has no model with which to evaluate a correct objection, and takes it for friction. The trap is sprung by the driver, which is exactly who this platform grades.

The objective gate did its job as an oracle, not as a difficulty. A separate control run — a fresh assistant, this same clone, one uncoached prompt, no learner in the loop — scores 13/13 (recorded in trap-manifest.md). So the grader is not what makes this hard. What the grader does is make the learner's shortcut visible and unarguable: 4/13 with a green 18-test suite is a sentence no amount of confidence survives. Without it, round 2 ships.

What the learner actually bought with good driving was not the score — it was the three things the score cannot see: they learned why their own suite was green, they stopped inventing customer-visible rules and asked, and they held the change to the ask. Grade the transcript. The oracle only tells you whether the work compiles.
